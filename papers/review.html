The Human Capital Consequences of an Earthquake

(with Rida Ali Khan) Journal of Human Capital, r&r
This paper uses the 2005 Pakistan earthquake as a natural experiment and estimates its long-term effects on human capital development. Exposure to the earthquake was plausibly exogenous for individuals born after the disaster. We exploit variation across birth cohorts and districts affected by the earthquake. We find that cohorts exposed to the earthquake exhibit higher rates of disabilities, lower educational attainment, and worse labor market outcomes. We also find that migration prior to the earthquake mitigated its impact on disabilities. The effects are more pronounced for cognitive disabilities, girls, and individuals from low-education and low-income households. These findings highlight the importance of migration and targeted investments in affected cohorts to improve long-term human capital outcomes.

Terrorism, Mental Health and Labor Market: Evidence from the 9/11 Attack

(with Chungeun Yoon)
This paper examines the impact of exposure to terrorism on mental health and labor market outcomes in the United States, focusing on the unexpected 9/11 attack. Using data from the Global Terrorism Database and the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System surveys, we find that exposure to terrorism is associated with diminished mental health, which in turn is associated with adverse labor market outcomes, including employment and income. However, we do not find any significant effects on physical health or overall health. Our findings show heterogeneous effects across gender, income, race, and ethnicity, with women and high-income individuals experiencing the most significant impacts. These results suggest the need for mental health interventions and economic support policies to mitigate the socioeconomic consequences of terrorism exposure.

Terrorism, Migration, and Labor Markets in Pakistan

This paper examines the economic consequences of terrorism in Pakistan using the variation between successful and failed attacks. Successful attacks, compared with failed attacks, are associated with a 6.1 percentage-point lower employment rate and approximately 0.86% lower earnings. The effect operates through migration-induced worker sorting, as residents of more-exposed districts relocate toward safer ones and reallocate labor away from exposed areas. The effects are larger in districts with stronger support for Sharia governance, weaker democratic preferences, and more extreme ideological views, and are particularly pronounced for women, rural and lower-resource households, and populations with high media exposure.

Dynamics of Backlash

Do counter-terrorism operations reduce political violence, or do they trigger strategic retaliation? Leveraging variation from the Red Mosque siege (2007), the killing of Osama bin Laden (2011), and Operation Zarbe-Azb (2014), I find that the earlier shocks generated immediate increases in attacks, casualties, and violence against law enforcement in the affected region, consistent with strategic retaliation. By contrast, Operation Zarb-e-Azb reduced aggregate violence but shifted the composition of remaining attacks toward state targets and the use of higher-technology weapons, consistent with capacity-constrained adaptation. Mechanism analysis points to capacity disruption, target substitution, and weapon upgrading as the main channels. The results are robust to the inverse hyperbolic sine transformation and to the Callaway– Sant’Anna staggered adoption estimator. Overall, falling attack counts after military operations may conceal a qualitative escalation in the residual militant threat.

How to Evaluate the Impact of E-Government Interventions?

E-government interventions have become a central instrument of publicsector reform, yet evidence on whether they deliver their intended effects remains methodologically underexplored. This paper systematically reviews the methods used to evaluate the causal impact of e-government interventions and develops practical guidance for matching methods to evaluation settings. Following systematic-review protocols, I screened 980 unique records and synthesized 47 studies, of which 12 are empirical causal evaluations of e-government programs. I organize the evidence around the potential-outcomes framework and assess each major design — randomized controlled trials, instrumental variables, difference-indifferences and its extensions, regression discontinuity, synthetic control, and propensity score matching — against the identification challenges that are characteristic of e-government: large-scale and phased geographic roll-out, non-random program placement driven by administrative and political factors, reliance on administrative and transaction data, and interference between units. I find that the egovernment evaluation still relies heavily on descriptive and design-weak approaches; that quasi-experimental designs are applied unevenly and often without adequate attention to identifying assumptions; and that randomized and discontinuity-based designs remain underused despite features of e-government roll-out that make them feasible. The paper contributes (i) a structured synthesis of how e-government impact has actually been evaluated, (ii) a comparative assessment that links identifying assumptions and data requirements to e-government settings, and (iii) a decision framework that maps the implementation characteristics of an intervention to appropriate evaluation designs. The framework is intended to help researchers and public managers commission and conduct more credible evaluations of digital government.
