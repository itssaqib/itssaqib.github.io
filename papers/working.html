Terrorism and Voting: Evidence from Death of Osama bin Laden

(with Chungeun Yoon & Inbok Rhee)
This paper examines the political consequences of terrorism in Pakistan. We exploit the death of Osama bin Laden as an instrumental variable for terrorist attacks and use quasi-random variation between successful and failed attacks across regions. Using candidate- and constituency-level electoral data, we find that successful terrorist attacks significantly increase the vote share of left-wing populist and independent candidates, while reducing support for right-wing parties. These findings suggest how terrorism can reshape the political landscape by shifting voter preferences toward populist alternatives.

The Impact of Terrorism on Perceptions of Income Inequality and Distributions

(with Chungeun Yoon & Inbok Rhee)
This paper examines the long-term effect of terrorism on perceptions of income inequality and distributions in Pakistan. We exploit the variation in age and location for the intensity of terror. Using data from the World Values Survey, combined with geocoded information on terrorist attacks in Pakistan, we examine individuals' early childhood exposure to terrorism between ages 0 and 5, a critical period for cognitive and preference development. We find that higher exposure to violence in childhood increases perceived income inequality as a critical social problem. The findings are more pronounced for females, unemployed individuals, low-income groups, and the Pashtun ethnic. The mechanisms driving these results include trust in institutions, gender equality, freedom of speech, and perceptions of government responsibility. These findings suggest that experiencing violence during formative years increases concern about income inequality.

Foreign Aid Cuts, Fertility, and Women’s Labor Supply: Evidence from the Mexico City Policy

(with Chungeun Yoon)
This paper examines how politically driven foreign aid cuts affect women's fertility, labor market participation, and empowerment in developing countries. Using the 2017 Trump expansion of the Mexico City Policy as a quasi-natural experiment, we exploit within-country variation in U.S. health aid exposure across Pakistani administrative divisions in a difference-in-differences framework. We find that the policy increased the likelihood of a birth in the past two years by 2.5 percentage points and reduced labor force participation by 10 percentage points, with monthly income falling by roughly 34\%. Women also lost household decision-making power over reproductive health. Effects concentrate among less-educated and rural women, suggesting the policy widened within-country inequality. Evidence on contraceptive sourcing points to disruption of NGO-linked distribution channels as the key mechanism. The findings highlight the broader socioeconomic costs of politically motivated aid restrictions.
